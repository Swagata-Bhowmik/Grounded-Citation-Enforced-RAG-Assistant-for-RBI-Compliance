🎯 The Problem & Business Case

CHAPTER 1 · WHYEvery project starts with a real problem. Here is the one Grounded solves — and why a wrong answer is expensive.

Why a bank would actually want this — and what it costs to get wrong.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

Imagine a giant rulebook with hundreds of pages of do's and don'ts. Every day, people at a bank have to answer “are we allowed to do this?” If they answer wrong, the bank can be fined. Reading the whole rulebook by hand every time is slow and easy to get wrong.

⚙️ The precise definition

RBI Master Directions are dense, cross-referenced regulatory texts. Compliance Q&A over them is a knowledge-intensive, high-stakes task: answers must be correct, traceable, and auditable, and a confidently wrong answer carries real regulatory and reputational cost.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Built an assistant that answers compliance questions over real RBI Master Directions
  • Every answer is grounded in retrieved regulation text and cites the exact page
  • It declines when the documents don't support an answer — the opposite of a chatbot that bluffs
  • Runs fully on-premise / offline — no bank data leaves the machine, no API bills

📌 A real example from our own data

A compliance analyst asks about KYC beneficial-ownership thresholds and gets the answer with the exact KYC Master Direction page to cite in their audit file — in seconds, not hours.

💼 Why a business cares

  • Audit-ready: page-level citations create a defensible paper trail
  • Risk reduction: enforced refusal removes the biggest danger — confident hallucination
  • Speed: analyst turnaround drops from manual page-hunting to seconds
  • Data privacy: fully offline — critical for banks that can't send data to external APIs
  • Zero ongoing cost: no per-query API fees; reproducible by anyone

🗂️ The Corpus (our data)

CHAPTER 2 · THE DATAWe have a problem. Step one of any RAG system: gather real, trustworthy documents — and check them before trusting them.

The real rulebooks we gave the assistant to read.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

We didn't make up any data. We gave the assistant a focused shelf of real RBI rulebooks — the kind a bank actually follows — and told it: only answer from these.

⚙️ The precise definition

A corpus of 10 real RBI Master Directions (born-digital PDFs from rbi.org.in) on a coherent theme. Each was profiled for text quality before use (page count, characters, scanned-vs-real text).

💼 Why a business cares

  • A coherent, cross-referencing corpus mirrors how real compliance questions span documents
  • Profiling first = trustworthy foundation; a scanned file would silently poison every answer

✂️ Chunking

CHAPTER 3 · PREPThe documents are huge. Before a computer can search them, we cut them into small, page-tagged pieces.

Cutting big documents into small, findable pieces.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

A 300-page book is too big to hand someone when they ask one question. So we cut each book into small index cards — each card holds one idea — and we write the page number on every card. Later, when someone asks something, we just grab the few right cards.

⚙️ The precise definition

Chunking splits documents into token-bounded, overlapping passages. Chunks must be small enough to fit the embedding model's input limit and focused enough that retrieval returns a tight piece of evidence rather than a whole document.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Chunk size 384 tokens with 64-token overlap, measured in the embedder's own tokenizer
  • Per-page chunking: every chunk is tagged with its exact page → the basis of page-level citations
  • We experimented with 256 / 384 / 480 tokens and chose 384 as the best balance (evidence, not a guess)
  • Result: 1,252 page-tagged chunks from 720 pages

📌 A real example from our own data

KYC page 22 becomes a 378-token chunk containing the opening of Chapter V, Customer Identification Procedure — a complete, self-contained idea, tagged ..._p22_....

💼 Why a business cares

  • Right-sized chunks = precise citations (a page, not a whole document)
  • The size experiment shows a defensible, measured engineering choice

🔢 Embeddings

CHAPTER 4 · MEANINGNow we have 1,252 small pieces. Next we give each one a numeric 'meaning fingerprint' so we can search by meaning, not just words.

Turning meaning into numbers so a computer can compare it.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

We give every card a “meaning fingerprint” — a list of numbers. Cards about similar things get similar fingerprints. So to find relevant cards, we just look for fingerprints close to the question's.

⚙️ The precise definition

An embedding is a dense vector (here 384 numbers) capturing a text's meaning. Similar meaning → vectors point in similar directions, compared by cosine similarity. This finds paraphrases that keyword search would miss.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Model: bge-small-en-v1.5 — small, CPU-friendly, fully offline, 384-dimensional
  • Vectors are L2-normalized; queries get a special instruction prefix (how bge was trained)
  • Embedded all 1,252 chunks on CPU — no GPU, no API
  • The 512-token limit of this model is exactly why chunks were capped under it

📌 A real example from our own data

A KYC question scored 0.978 similarity to a near-identical restatement, 0.721 to a same-topic answer, and only 0.546 to unrelated priority-sector text — the ranking is exactly right.

💼 Why a business cares

  • Meaning-based search finds the right rule even when the user's words differ from the regulation's

🗄️ Vector Store (ChromaDB)

CHAPTER 5 · STORAGEEvery piece has a fingerprint. We need a store that, given a question, returns the closest pieces instantly.

A filing cabinet that finds cards by meaning in milliseconds.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

Once every card has a fingerprint, we need a smart filing cabinet that, given a question's fingerprint, instantly hands back the closest cards. That's a vector database.

⚙️ The precise definition

A vector database indexes embeddings for fast nearest-neighbour search. Unlike a normal database (exact matches), it finds items by closeness in meaning space, and persists to disk.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Used ChromaDB with cosine distance, persisted locally (survives restarts)
  • Stored each chunk's vector + text + metadata (document, page) — metadata is what makes a citation
  • We pass in our own embeddings (one consistent model for chunks and queries — avoids a classic RAG bug)

📌 A real example from our own data

Asking “beneficial owner threshold for a trust” returns KYC pages 38, 6, 40 in milliseconds — each carrying its page number for citation.

🔎 Hybrid Retrieval (BM25 + semantic)

CHAPTER 6 · SEARCHMeaning-search alone misses exact clause numbers. So we add a second search — by keyword — and merge the two.

Two search helpers — one for exact words, one for meaning — working together.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

One helper is like Ctrl+F: great at finding exact words and code numbers. The other understands meaning. Exact-number questions need the first; paraphrased questions need the second. So we use both and merge results.

⚙️ The precise definition

Hybrid retrieval combines BM25 (keyword scoring) with semantic (embedding) search, fused by Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) — which merges by rank, so the two very different score scales don't clash.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Built a BM25 keyword index over all 1,252 chunks, tokenized to keep clause numbers like 13.03 intact
  • Ran semantic + BM25 in parallel and fused with RRF (robust, parameter-light)
  • This is why the corpus was chosen: regulation is full of exact clause numbers where keywords beat pure semantics

📌 A real example from our own data

A user searching the exact acronym “KFS” or a clause number gets the literal match from BM25, while a paraphrased question is caught by semantic search — the fusion covers both.

💼 Why a business cares

  • Compliance users search by exact regulation numbers constantly — hybrid is not optional for this domain

🎯 Cross-Encoder Re-ranking

CHAPTER 7 · PRECISIONFast search gives a rough shortlist. Now a slower, sharper model re-checks the top candidates with the question in hand.

A careful second reader who re-checks the shortlist with the question in hand.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

The first search grabs maybe 50 likely cards fast. Then a slower, smarter reader looks at each card together with the question and decides how good it really is, putting the best on top.

⚙️ The precise definition

A cross-encoder reads the (query, passage) pair jointly and outputs one relevance score — far more accurate than comparing two separate vectors, but too slow for all chunks, so it only re-ranks the ~50 candidates.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Model: ms-marco-MiniLM cross-encoder; positive score ≈ relevant, negative ≈ irrelevant
  • Two-stage design: cheap hybrid search narrows 1,252 → ~50, then the cross-encoder re-ranks those
  • The score's size is a confidence signal we reuse for the refusal decision

📌 A real example from our own data

For the KFS question, the true answer (Digital Lending page 8) scored +4.95 while the next candidates fell below +0.3 — a decisive, confident top result.

🛡️ Citation Enforcement (the refusal)

CHAPTER 8 · TRUSTWe have the best evidence and a confidence score. Here is the rule that decides: answer with citations, or honestly refuse.

The assistant says “I don't know” instead of making things up.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

If the best card still isn't a good match for the question, the honest thing is to say “I don't have this” — not invent an answer. That refusal is the single most important behaviour for a compliance tool.

⚙️ The precise definition

Citation enforcement gates answering on the top re-rank score: below a threshold, the system declines. The threshold and all prompts live in a versioned config file (not hard-coded), so policy is tracked in Git.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Threshold set at 0.0 in prompts/prompts.yaml (the cross-encoder's natural boundary)
  • Answerable questions scored ≥ +2.1; an out-of-scope question topped out at −7.4 — a huge, safe margin
  • Refusal message and system prompt are versioned (we bumped v1→v2 to fix citation formatting, zero code change)

📌 A real example from our own data

“How is a chocolate cake prepared?” → best evidence −7.41 → declines. The KFS question → +4.95 → answers with citations. Same system, opposite, correct decisions.

💼 Why a business cares

  • A confident wrong answer is worse than “not found” in compliance — refusal is the feature
  • Threshold-as-config lets a risk team tune strictness without engineering

🧠 The LangGraph Agent

CHAPTER 9 · THE BRAINRetrieval, the refusal rule, and the writer need a conductor. Enter the agent that decides, acts, and double-checks.

A little decision-maker that chooses: search, answer, or politely decline — then double-checks.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

Instead of always doing the same steps, a small “brain” decides what to do: get evidence, judge if it's strong enough, then either write a cited answer or refuse — and finally check that it really did cite a page.

⚙️ The precise definition

A LangGraph state machine: retrieve → assess → (answer | decline) → verify → cite. The conditional answer/decline edge is the genuine agentic decision; a local LLM writes the grounded answer.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Answers are written by a local LLM (Qwen2.5-3B, quantized, offline) via llama.cpp — zero API cost
  • The verify node confirms the written answer actually contains a page citation
  • Provider-swappable: the local model could be swapped for a hosted one without touching the agent

📌 A real example from our own data

The KFS question flows: retrieve → strong evidence → LLM writes the answer citing [02_Digital_Lending_Directions_2025.pdf p.8] → verify=True. The cake question stops at “assess” and declines — the LLM is never even called.

💼 Why a business cares

  • The decision flow makes behaviour predictable and auditable — you can point to exactly why it answered or declined

📊 Evaluation (the golden set)

CHAPTER 10 · PROOFThe machine works. But does it work WELL? Time to grade it against a hand-made answer key.

A graded pop-quiz with an answer key.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

To know if the assistant is actually good, we wrote a quiz with known correct answers and graded it — including trick questions it should refuse.

⚙️ The precise definition

A golden set of hand-authored questions with known-correct sources. Two LLM-free, deterministic metrics: retrieval recall@5 (is the right document in the top-5?) and refusal accuracy (answer in-scope, decline out-of-scope).

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • 26 questions: 24 answerable (each mapped to its correct document) + 2 out-of-scope refusal tests
  • Recall@5 = 1.0 and Refusal accuracy = 1.0
  • Both metrics are fast & deterministic → perfect for an automated quality gate

📌 A real example from our own data

For “priority sector lending target as % of ANBC”, the correct Priority Sector Lending document appears in the top results; the cake question is correctly declined.

💼 Why a business cares

  • Honest caveat: these are strong partly because the set is small and recall is document-level. It's a defensible baseline, and the CI gate exists to catch regressions as the set grows — no inflation.

🔬 NLI Faithfulness Scoring

CHAPTER 11 · FACT-CHECKRecall and refusal look great. One more check: did the written answer add any claim the evidence didn't support?

Checking the assistant didn't add anything that isn't in the cards.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

Even with the right cards, a model might slip in a detail that isn't actually there. So we check, sentence by sentence, whether the evidence really supports each claim — like a fact-checker.

⚙️ The precise definition

Faithfulness = is every claim entailed by the retrieved evidence? We score it with a Natural Language Inference (NLI) model (entailment / neutral / contradiction) — offline, zero cost, no LLM judge.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • Model: nli-distilroberta; each answer sentence checked against context sentences for entailment
  • We caught and fixed a real bug: feeding whole passages made scores mushy — sentence-level premises fixed it
  • Grounded answer scored 0.967; a fabricated answer scored ~0.01 — sharp separation

📌 A real example from our own data

A real grounded answer scored 0.97; a fake one about “free airline miles on every loan” scored 0.01 — the scorer cleanly flags invention.

💼 Why a business cares

  • An offline, free faithfulness check means quality can be measured continuously without API cost
Faithfulness: grounded vs hallucinated answer (hover for exact score)

🚦 CI/CD Quality Gate

CHAPTER 12 · GUARDRAILQuality is proven today. This robot guard makes sure it STAYS proven on every future code change.

A robot guard that re-runs the quiz on every change and blocks anything that makes it worse.

🧒 The intuition (plain words)

Whenever we change the code, a robot automatically re-runs the quiz. If the score drops below the bar, it blocks the change. So quality can't silently break.

⚙️ The precise definition

Continuous Integration: a GitHub Actions workflow runs the fast metrics on every push via pytest and fails the build if recall or refusal drop below 0.85.

🔧 What WE actually built (specifics & numbers)

  • scripts/quality_gate.py + tests/test_quality_gate.py enforce the thresholds
  • .github/workflows/quality-gate.yml runs it automatically on every push / PR
  • Rebuilds the retriever from the committed corpus on a clean machine — true reproducibility

📌 A real example from our own data

If a future change dropped recall to 0.7, the build would turn red and the change couldn't merge — quality is guaranteed, not hoped for.

💼 Why a business cares

  • This is the line between “worked on my laptop once” and a system with continuously guaranteed quality
